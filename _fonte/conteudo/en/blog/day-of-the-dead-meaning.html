Día de Muertos, or Day of the Dead, is one of Mexico’s best-known traditions. It is often mixed up with Halloween because it falls at the same time of year, but it means something different: not a night of scares, but a time to remember, with affection, the people who have died. This guide explains what it is, when it happens and what each element means, with care and only what sources confirm.

When is Day of the Dead?

In Mexico, Día de Muertos is observed on November 1 and 2. Families gather to remember loved ones who have passed. The days fall alongside All Saints’ Day (November 1) and All Souls’ Day (November 2) on the Catholic calendar, but they are not the same thing: Día de Muertos has Indigenous roots and its own way of being celebrated.

The ofrenda: an altar of memory

At the center of the tradition is the ofrenda, an altar that each family builds at home or at the cemetery. Among the most common elements:

  • Photos of the people being remembered.
  • Candles that light the altar.
  • Pan de muerto, a sweet bread made for the occasion.
  • Cempasúchil flowers, a bright orange marigold.

Each family chooses what to place. The idea is that the altar gathers what reminds them of the person, so every ofrenda tells a different story.

Cempasúchil: the “twenty flowers”

The orange flower on the ofrenda is called cempasúchil. The word comes from Nahuatl, from cempohualxochitl, which translates as “twenty flowers”. In English it is usually called the Mexican marigold.

Calaveritas literarias

Another mark of the season is the calaveritas literarias: rhymed verses, often humorous, in which Death is the main character. The humor belongs to the genre, and Death appears as a character rather than a forbidden subject.

What UNESCO recognized

In 2008, UNESCO inscribed the “Indigenous festivities dedicated to the dead” of Mexico on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. Note the precision: the inscription refers to the Indigenous festivities dedicated to the dead, not to the whole of Día de Muertos, which has also absorbed influences from other traditions over time.

Quick questions

Is Day of the Dead the “Mexican Halloween”?

No. They are different traditions with different roots that happen to fall on nearby dates. Día de Muertos is a time of memory and family.

What does cempasúchil mean?

It comes from the Nahuatl cempohualxochitl, “twenty flowers”. It is the orange flower used on the ofrenda.

What are calaveritas literarias?

Rhymed verses, often humorous, in which Death is the main character.
