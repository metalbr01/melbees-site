GTA VI is out, and anyone who bought it knows the scene: a progress bar that crawls, an install that asks for one more update, a game that will not open yet, and a strong wish to already be inside. On our social channels we joked about it in a short video: while the file downloads, Mel solves the Daily Challenge on her phone. The joke has a useful core. There is a good way to fill those waiting minutes, and it fits in five.

Why a 5-minute game works for waiting

Waiting on a download or a queue does not call for a long game. It calls for something that starts fast, ends fast and does not trap you in an hour-long session. A 5-minute game has three advantages here:

  • It starts right away. No install, no account, no update. The Melbees Daily Challenge opens in the browser on your phone or computer.
  • It ends by itself. There is one challenge per day. When you finish, you are finished, and the download bar is exactly where you left it.
  • It warms up your head. A logic game gets you thinking, which helps you arrive at the big game a little sharper.

This is true for GTA VI and for any big launch. The size of these installs has become a running joke (in our video the bar read 150 GB, purely for comedy). The point is only that small, good, free games exist for the in-between moments.

What Melbees is, in a few lines

Melbees is Hangman crossed with Minesweeper on a honeycomb. You uncover secret words, the Secrets, but the letters are not laid out in an alphabet: they are scattered across hexes. On each hex you pick one of two actions. Match means "this letter is in the Secret". Discard means "it is not". Both open the hex and show the truth: a flower if the letter is right, or a number if it is not. That number is the Pollen, and it counts how many right letters sit on the neighboring hexes. A wrong action costs a petal.

The loading screen tip: how to read the Pollen

Big games like to show tips on loading screens. We made our own, and it doubles as a summary for newcomers: each Pollen number counts the right letters in the hexes next to it. A hex has at most six neighbors, and fewer on the edge. From there you can reason like this:

  • A 0 means no neighbor is a right letter. The game opens those neighbors for you, so you gain ground without losing a petal.
  • A number above zero, with few neighbors still closed, tells you where the right letters are hiding.
  • Crossing the Pollen of two neighboring hexes often reveals what a single number cannot.
Question: a hex shows 1 and only one neighbor is still closed. What do you do with that neighbor?

Match it. The 1 says there is one right letter nearby, and if every other neighbor is already open with no flower, it can only be in the one that remains.

A plan for dead time

  1. Start the download and open the Daily Challenge in your browser.
  2. Pick a level. Beginner is good for day one, Expert for when you have the hang of it.
  3. Start with the zeros and read the numbers as you go.
  4. When you finish, close the tab and go back to the big game. Tomorrow's challenge only shows up tomorrow.

That one-a-day rule is on purpose. Melbees wants to fit into your routine, not compete with the 100 hours of a giant game. It is the appetizer, not the main course.
