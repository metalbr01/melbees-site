Every year at the end of October, Halloween shows up in shop windows, classrooms and feeds. Many people call it an American holiday, and in part it is, because the United States gave it the shape it has today. But the story begins much earlier, across the Atlantic. Here is a short guide to what is known, sticking to what sources agree on.

The beginning: Samhain, a Celtic festival

The root most often cited is Samhain, a festival of the Celtic peoples of ancient Ireland and Britain. It marked the end of the harvest and the start of winter, at the turn from October 31 into November 1. On that night, the dead were said to return home. That idea, a night when the worlds of the living and the dead sit closer together, explains much of the mood we still associate with Halloween.

A note of caution: sources disagree on whether the festival fell on November 1 or from sunset on October 31 to sunset on November 1, so “the turn of October 31 into November 1” covers both. Many details about its pre-Christian rites also come from much later accounts, so treat them as stories, not facts.

Where the name comes from

Halloween is a Scottish short form of All Hallows’ Even: the evening before All Saints’ Day, on November 1. Hallow is an old word for “saint”, and even means evening. Over time the phrase was worn down into one word. Etymology references such as Etymonline and Merriam-Webster record this origin.

The first lantern was not a pumpkin

Today the symbol of Halloween is the carved pumpkin, the jack-o’-lantern. The first lanterns were something else: in Ireland and Scotland, people carved faces into turnips and potatoes and set them in windows to scare off spirits.

How the pumpkin got into the story

The swap happened in America. Irish immigrants brought the custom to the United States in the 19th century and traded the turnip for the pumpkin, which is native to the Americas, bigger and easier to carve. It caught on so well that most people now assume a Halloween lantern is always a pumpkin. History.com and Clemson University’s extension service tell the same story.

“Trick or treat”: since when?

The oldest known printed use of the phrase trick or treat, in that order, is from 1927, in a newspaper in Alberta, Canada. That does not mean the custom began that year, only that it is the earliest printed record found. Earlier variants such as “treat or trick” exist, and sources disagree on the newspaper and town, so we leave those details out.

Quick quiz

Was the first jack-o’-lantern a pumpkin?

No. In Ireland and Scotland the first ones were carved from turnips and potatoes. The pumpkin came later, in the United States.

Key takeaways

  • Halloween traces back to Samhain, a Celtic festival at the turn of October 31 into November 1.
  • The name is a contraction of All Hallows’ Even.
  • The lantern began as a turnip and became a pumpkin in the United States in the 19th century.
  • The oldest known printed trick or treat is from 1927, in Canada.
