I've never played Minecraft, but I fell down the RNG hole

My daughter plays Minecraft, I don't. A Summoning Salt video and a Veritasium one pulled me into RNG: in a computer's generator, the same seed replays the same sequence.

By Melby Dev · Published on November 22, 2026 · Updated on November 22, 2026 · 4 min read

In a computer's random generator, the same seed replays the same sequence.

Pow

My daughter plays Minecraft. I've never really played it. She likes the style of the game and the freedom to create, and when I asked what she sees in it, she didn't even pause: "I like Minecraft because it has lots of game mods, cute skins. I think the idea of the game is cool and I think it's really well made. And it encourages creativity. It lets me create things I can't create in real life."

That line stuck with me. It's why I opened Summoning Salt's new video, Minecraft Speedrunning: A Brief History, published November 14 and running 1h13. I wanted to see the other side of the game, the one where people race a clock. The video left me wanting to dig into RNG, the "random" a computer makes.

The word I couldn't shake: seed

On speedrun.com, the Minecraft: Java Edition board splits runs by seed type. On a Set Seed run, the runner picks and knows the world before the attempt. On a Random Seed run, the world comes from the game's own roll. Two of today's records:

  • Any%, Set Seed, External, version 1.9+: 31.852 seconds of in-game time, set on December 22, 2025. See the run.
  • Any%, Random Seed, External, version 1.9+: the best time I found on the board is 3 min 51.965 s of in-game time, set on January 3, 2024. See the run.
  • All Advancements: 13:46.37 of in-game time, set on September 5, 2026, on a fixed seed (versions 1.1 to 1.6). See the run.

A bit over thirty seconds on one side, almost four minutes on the other. It's tempting to say the seed explains it all, but it doesn't. These are two Any% subcategories with different versions and conditions, so the two times don't isolate the effect of the seed. All I can say is that with a known world, a runner can drill the same route until their fingers know it.

Then Veritasium showed up

A few days later, on the 18th, the Veritasium channel posted Randomness is a lie. Let me explain, about 39 minutes long. Going by the chapters the channel wrote in its own description, it opens with "The Hack That Was Stumping Casinos" (the hack that had casinos stumped), goes through "Finding Minecraft Diamonds With Math" and linear congruential generators, then "How Russian Hackers Cracked the Mersenne Twister", and ends on certified quantum randomness, with "Cloudflare's Lava Lamps" along the way. The description also thanks the person who showed them a Minecraft exploit and credits a team at NIST for the quantum experiment.

I read that list and thought: that's my question, just told by people who know. Casinos, Minecraft and the word "random" in one lineup.

Same seed, same sequence

A computer doesn't roll dice. A pseudorandom generator starts from a number, the seed, and runs a formula that spits out a sequence that looks like luck. Reuse the seed and the same sequence comes back. That's what the documentation for Python's random module says.

Predicting what comes next is another story. To guess the next numbers from the outside, you have to know or rebuild the generator's internal state. And interfering with a game or a machine is a separate step on top of that. The Mersenne Twister, which Python uses, is fully deterministic, and the docs themselves say it's not meant for security or cryptography. That doesn't prove any casino can be exploited. It only says this kind of generator wasn't built to protect anything.

I won't pretend I followed every step of the video. But I got that part.

What if the source isn't a formula?

In March 2025, Nature published Certified randomness using a trapped-ion quantum processor (volume 640, pages 343 to 348; the open version is on arXiv). The team came from JPMorganChase and Quantinuum, with US national labs and the University of Texas at Austin. It ran on a 56-qubit quantum computer, the Quantinuum H2-1, and the authors certify 71,313 bits of entropy, under specific assumptions and limits: a restricted kind of adversary plus some extra assumptions, treating the quantum computer as a server you don't trust. According to the bank's announcement, it's a first step, and the authors themselves note limits for immediate real-world use.

It's still research, not something off a shelf. But the shift is what hooks me: from "a formula that looks like luck" to "a physical process you can't replay."
